# The copy layer

Hallmark keeps the facts honest: no invented metric, quote or logo, no copy
tells (gate 19), no eyebrow, about 80 words a screen. It says less about what
the words should say and how they should sound, and that is where generated
pages still give themselves away. A model writes every client in one voice,
warm, wry, modest and a little literary, and reuses the same devices in every
section. A garage and a nail salon end up sounding alike.

The cure is facts and decisions, never synonyms. A word swapped for its
synonym becomes the next tell.

## On a redesign, the words stay

Hallmark's redesign keeps every word unless a gate names it (`redesign.md`,
"The words"). Each estate redesign brief also keeps the copy's meaning until
the owner approves a change on the redesign board. On a redesign this layer
therefore covers three things only:

- lines a copy gate names, with each change listed before and after;
- microcopy the app lacks (empty states, errors, confirmations), proposed on
  the board;
- the voice of any new line the owner asks for.

On a new page it covers everything.

## 1. The fact sheet

Before the first sentence, write down what the brief, the client's files or
the old site give, word for word where you can:

- what it is, in one plain sentence;
- who it is for, and who should look elsewhere;
- what people do today instead;
- the true difference, with its proof;
- the customers' own words, from reviews or messages;
- numbers, each with where it came from;
- the people the brief names;
- odd true details: an object, a habit, a date, the awkward bit;
- one limit, such as closed on Mondays, cash only, or Android only;
- the offer: price, booking terms, the one action.

An empty field stays empty. Its section shrinks or goes, and the handoff lists
what the owner must supply. Nothing is invented (Hallmark's Floor rule 8, gate
46a). A thin brief still gets the best page its facts allow.

## 2. The voice card

Every client gets a voice of its own, written on the plan in a few lines and
never shown on the page:

```
Voice: plain facts
Person: "we" for the work, "you" for what the visitor gets
Sentences: mostly 8 to 16 words; one longer one where it explains
Humour: none in the hero, the prices or the forms
House words: the client's own nouns (flatbed, winch, recovery), the same word every time
Never: adjectives about ourselves, a pun on the name
```

Choose the voice by the buyer, the price and the client's real character. If
the brief, an old site or a writing sample has a voice already, copy that
first. The examples below are made up for this page.

| Voice | Sounds like | Suits | Fails when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plain facts | "Notary office on the main square. Deeds, wills and powers of attorney, Monday to Friday, 9 to 17." | advice, health, a parish's notices | the facts are thin and it reads like an empty CV |
| Technical, with proof | "Every request waits in one queue. The next one starts the moment the last one ends." | software, tools | it turns into a spec list with no problem named |
| The owner speaking | "We started towing in 2009 because the nearest truck was an hour away." | a small business with a real story in the brief | the story is put on |
| Sensory, present tense | "You sit by the window; the lamp stays on until the last coat dries." | food, body and care, stays | every section turns sensory; prices and booking stay plain |
| Narrow and firm | "We recover cars and vans up to 3.5 tonnes. Not lorries." | workshops, specialists | the refusal is invented to sound focused |
| Product first | "The page shows who is served now and who waits, every two seconds." | apps, hardware | a turn of phrase has no fact under it |
| Places and dates | "The church on the hill, rebuilt in 1931. Sunday service at 10." | heritage, a parish, a guesthouse | there is no real place or history in the brief |
| Light and playful | "Glitter is free. Removing it is not." | shops, small brands | the joke is on the customer, or the purchase is money or health |

- **The other-trades test.** Picture the voice card on nine businesses in other
  trades. If it suits them, push it further toward this client.
- **Voice holds, tone moves.** The hero and the story carry most of the voice.
  Prices, forms, errors and questions go plain, because readers there are
  anxious.

## 3. The shape follows the reader's questions

A visitor asks, in roughly this order: what is this, is it for me, why should
I believe you, how does it work, what does it cost, what if it goes wrong, and
what do I do now. A section exists only when the fact sheet has material for
it. Counts follow the content: two real plans are two cards, one quote is one
quote, and no quote is a normal page. This pairs with Hallmark's `anatomy.md`,
which gives each job its own sections.

## 4. Drafting moves

- Put a fact where an adjective wants to go. Adjectives that stay are physical
  or measurable: heated, 3.5 tonnes, ground floor.
- Prefer proper nouns to categories: the street, the make of lift, the tool's
  name.
- Explain how it works in verb-led steps.
- Make one admission a page, and only from the brief.
- Put numbers in sentences, with their unit, uneven where real.
- A button is a verb and the real object: "Call the tow line", "Book a fitting".
- Write the microcopy in the same voice. An error names the field and what to
  type, the sent line names what was sent, and an empty state says what
  belongs there and the one action that fills it.
- One name for each thing, every time.

## 5. The device budget

Every device works once a page. Used by default, it becomes a tell.

| Device | Per page |
|---|---|
| A heading in two fragments ("Two rooms. One view.") | 1 |
| "X, not Y" and its cousins (rather than, instead of, without the) | 2 in all |
| A negation run ("No forms. No waiting.") | 1, and only when each item is a refusal a buyer can check |
| A dry closing line on a paragraph | 2, never in a row |
| A pun on the business's name | 1, never the main heading |
| Virtue words about ourselves (honest, calm, quiet, genuinely, simply) | 0 |
| A strip of figures | only facts from the brief |
| A list of three | only when there are three things |

Hallmark already fails the buzzwords, the dashes and the stock second action
(gate 19), the echo of one fact in four sections (gate 69) and middle-dot
strings (gate 63).

## 6. Checks

1. **The rival test.** Put a competitor's name, or the same trade in another
   town, into the main heading and each section heading. A line that still
   reads true says nothing about this client, so rewrite it from the fact
   sheet.
2. **Read it aloud as the owner of the business.** Cut every line they would
   not say about themselves.
3. **Look for the replacement habit.** After the fixes, check what took the
   removed habit's place: every sentence a fragment, "rather than" everywhere,
   everything in pairs.
4. **Several pages in one run**, such as the estate board: read their headings
   side by side. A cadence they share is a tic.
5. **A fresh reader**, when a subagent is free. Give it only the text and ask
   what this is, who it is for, why this one, what it costs and what would make
   a visitor hesitate.

All of this applies in the page's own language. The budget counts that
language's equivalents of each device.
